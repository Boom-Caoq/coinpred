# I Stopped Fading Every RSI 70 Print. This Is the Daily Habit I Use Instead

The first time most traders meet RSI, they are handed a switch: sell at 70, buy at 30. Crypto punishes that switch. I still use RSI almost every day. I just use it as a thermometer — a reading of how hot recent swings have been — and I refuse to let a single touch of a round number place the order.

This is the habit I actually run, and the places I have watched people get the thermometer wrong.

## What the number is saying

In plain language, RSI asks whether recent ups or recent downs have been stronger, then compresses the answer into a 0–100 scale. Near 100, almost everything recent was up, and it was fast. Near 0, the opposite. Near 50, the two sides are roughly in balance.

The conventional bands — above 70 overbought, below 30 oversold, 40–60 as a middling zone — are conventions, not physics. Crypto is volatile enough that some people move the rails to 80 and 20. I usually leave the rails at 70 and 30 so I am looking at the same default most platforms show, and I change my behavior around those rails instead of constantly retuning the indicator until history looks tidy.

A schematic in your head is enough: a line wandering through a hot band, a cold band, and a wide middle. In live markets it pokes in and out constantly. That is why the first kiss of 70 is almost never a reason to act.

I also care about the formula when I compare notes with someone else. Most platforms default to Wilder’s smoothing. A simple-moving-average variant will print slightly different numbers. If we are going to argue about a 34 versus a 31, we should at least be computing the same object.

## Overbought can mean the trend is strong

The beginner trap is “RSI is over 70, so fade it.” A clear uptrend can keep the reading parked in overbought for many sessions while price continues to travel. I watched that happen in August 2026, when Bitcoin left a mid-summer range and RSI stayed above 70 for a long stretch — well over a week of daily bars — while price still pushed higher. I will not reprint a date-by-date table. The lesson does not live in a single print. It lives in the duration.

In a trend, an overbought reading often means “this move is violent and persistent,” not “the top is here.” I become more interested when RSI falls back under 70 and price also loses a zone I had already marked. The exit from the extreme, plus the structure, is the story. The first tag is just the temperature going red.

The mirror image is oversold. A crash through 30 means the drop was sharp. It does not mean the floor is in. I have watched daily RSI sink to extremely low teens and still see price probe lower for several more sessions. The cleaner reaction, when it came, showed up after the oversold condition had persisted and price had stopped making a fresh extreme, and only in company with a pre-drawn support zone and a candle I was willing to respect. Even then, a later bounce is a hindsight observation, not a target I could have written down on the first oversold close.

So the rule I actually use is: extremes describe speed, not arrival. The first break of 30 often still sees a second probe. I care more about reaction inside the extreme, and about whether price is sitting on a level I already cared about, than about the raw number.

## Divergence is a warning I refuse to treat as a trigger

The dramatic RSI story is divergence: price makes a higher high, RSI makes a lower high, and upside energy looks weaker than last time. The bullish version is the mirror.

I use that as a warning. It says this push is not as strong as the previous one. It does not say “sell tomorrow.” In the early autumn of 2026, Bitcoin’s swing highs in the upper 80,000s arrived with a cooler RSI than the prior peak. That is a textbook sketch. It was still not a timestamp. Some divergences fade quickly; some chop for weeks; some are cancelled by a new high. If the market is still holding a key average or a support I trust, I am more likely to tighten risk than to flip from a thermometer.

That is the misunderstanding I see most often: people treat a divergence as if it were a scheduled reversal. It is closer to a change in the quality of the move. Quality can deteriorate for a long time before price actually gives you a level to act against.

## The mid-band is where range traders should actually look

People memorize 70 and 30 and then sit on their hands through an entire range, because RSI never gives them a classic extreme. That is not a market failure. In a mid-2026 summer stretch, when Bitcoin spent weeks chopping inside a relatively tight band, daily RSI lived almost entirely in the 40–60 area. Price still swept the range more than once.

If I only wait for 30 or 70, I will miss the work that actually happens at the range floor and ceiling. In chop, I want to see buyer reactions near support and seller reactions near resistance, and I let RSI confirm that neither side is overheated. The thermometer is doing its job when it stays boring.

A later cooling — for example a drop that takes RSI from the mid-40s into the mid-30s without yet crossing 30 — I read as “clearly cooler,” not as a buy signal. Whether it later tags 30 or stabilizes in the 30–40 band is for future closes to answer.

## Period, timeframe, and the habit

Fourteen is a convention that dates back to Wilder’s examples, not a magic constant. RSI(7) turns fast and throws off more noise. RSI(21) is slower and better at filtering. My habit on the daily is to keep 14 on the main pane and glance at 21 for confirmation. I rarely decide from RSI(7) on the daily alone unless I am also managing a lower-timeframe entry.

The five steps I actually repeat:

1. Trend first. Overbought and oversold mean different things in an uptrend, a downtrend, and a range.
2. Zone next. Is the reading hot, cold, or hanging around 40–60?
3. Check whether price swings and RSI swings agree.
4. Wait for a close, the next candle, and the location against support or resistance.
5. Write the invalidation. If the idea is wrong — a daily close back through a zone, for example — what do I do?

The common mistakes are all versions of skipping that order: treating 70/30 as a switch, reading RSI without a map of levels, forcing a daily conclusion from a five-minute divergence, ignoring the mid-band, or retuning the period until the past looks perfect.

When daily RSI and a four-hour RSI disagree, I defer to the higher timeframe. Daily still trending up, with the four-hour merely overbought, usually looks like a short pullback to me. Daily already broken, with the four-hour oversold, is not automatically a major bottom.

## How I hold this next to a short-horizon model

If I also look at a short-horizon directional model, I still want the daily temperature first. A model leaning lower on a fifteen-minute window, while the daily is an intact uptrend with RSI merely cooling from overbought, is often a pullback story rather than a regime change. The reverse is also true: a short-horizon bounce call while the daily has already broken and RSI is still falling is not automatically a major bottom.

I do not auto-trade an extreme, and I do not read model confidence as a win rate. If the model is neutral and RSI is sitting in the mid-band of a range, I do not invent a 70/30 event so I have something to do. Sitting out is the correct use of a thermometer that says “neither hot nor cold.”

The same discipline applies if I am considering a short-dated directional ticket. An overbought daily in a strong trend is not, by itself, a reason to buy Lower. An oversold print against a broken daily structure is not, by itself, a reason to buy Higher. I want the window, the settlement rule, and the level to be written down first. If I cannot name the invalidation, the RSI number is just a decoration.

RSI is not investment advice, and it is not a promise that an oversold print will pay. Crypto can stay extreme longer than a rule of thumb can tolerate. Event contracts and other short-dated products can lose the entire stake. I size as if I can be wrong on the first tag — because I often am.
